## Per-claim detail

### C1 — e1RM via Epley formula  
*(Priority: High)*

- **Formula-check:** Reproduced numerically: e1RM = weight*(1+reps/30). Confirmed reps=1 does NOT return weight exactly (gives weight*1.0333, i.e. a 3.3% overestimate) -- the app's stated special-case rule 'if reps=1, e1RM=weight' is a deliberate override of the raw Epley formula, not a property of the formula itself. Cross-checked against Brzycki (w*36/(37-reps)) and Lombardi (w*reps^0.10): all three agree closely for reps<=5 (within ~2-5%) and diverge sharply above ~10-12 reps -- e.g. at 20 reps Epley=166.7, Brzycki=211.8, Lombardi=134.9 (base weight=100), a >50% spread between formulas, confirming error grows large at high rep counts.
- **Verified source(s):**
  - Epley, B. (1985). Poundage Chart. Boyd Epley Workout. Lincoln, NE: Body Enterprises, p.86. — no DOI (non-peer-reviewed trade publication/coaching handout)
    - *Verification:* Verified as a real, widely-cited source across many peer-reviewed papers' reference lists (e.g. LeSuer et al. 1997; Nature Sci Cord 2017 review; JSCR 2006 Reynolds/Gordon/Robergs). Confirmed the formula form 1RM=w(1+reps/30) is consistently attributed to this exact source across independent validation studies -- attribution is real, but original source itself is an uncontrolled coaching poundage chart, not an empirical study with a reported methodology or sample.
  - LeSuer DA, McCormick JH, Mayhew JL, Wasserstein RL, Arnold MD (1997). The accuracy of prediction equations for estimating 1-RM performance in the bench press, squat, and deadlift. J Strength Cond Res 11(4):211-213. — cited via Nature Sci Cord 2017 reference list
    - *Verification:* Verified via citation in multiple later reviews; content matches claim -- tested 7 equations on untrained college students across bench/squat/deadlift, found all equations significantly underestimated deadlift 1RM, exercise-specific accuracy differences confirmed.
  - Reynolds JM, Gordon TJ, Robergs RA (2006). Prediction of one repetition maximum strength from multiple repetition maximum testing and anthropometry. J Strength Cond Res 20(3):584-592. — PMID not directly retrieved, but full text located at unm.edu/~rrobergs (author's institutional page), matches title/journal/year
    - *Verification:* Verified — content matches claim that prediction accuracy degrades at higher rep ranges and 5RM data gave best accuracy.
  - Whisenant, Panton, East, Broeder (2003) and Ware et al. (1995), squat-specific validation studies cited secondarily via OpenSIUC thesis and Nature/Spinal Cord review — not independently confirmed (secondary citation only)
    - *Verification:* Partially verified — found only via citation chains in other papers (OpenSIUC thesis, Nature Spinal Cord review), not retrieved directly; content (squat RTF 11-25 'not acceptable') is consistent across multiple independent citing sources, increasing confidence, but treat as medium-verified.
- **Evidence level:** C   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** Accuracy is good (average error often <5-8%) only for reps <=~10, and best in the 1-6 rep range; error grows substantially and unpredictably above 10 reps. All equations including Epley have been shown to significantly underestimate deadlift 1RM and to perform worse for squat than for bench press (LeSuer et al. 1997; Ware et al. 1995 called Epley 'not acceptable' for squat when reps-to-failure ranged 11-25). Accuracy also degrades in older adults and untrained/novice lifters, and assumes the submaximal set was taken to genuine momentary failure -- if reps are stopped short of failure (common in app-logged 'working sets'), e1RM will be systematically underestimated regardless of formula. Epley tends toward slightly higher (more optimistic) 1RM estimates than Brzycki only in the roughly 1-9 rep range (e.g. reps=8: Epley=126.7 vs Brzycki=124.1, base weight=100); the two are approximately equal near reps=10 (133.3 vs 133.3), and above ~10 reps this reverses -- Brzycki's estimate grows substantially higher and more steeply than Epley's (reps=15: 163.6 vs 150.0; reps=20: 211.8 vs 166.7; reps=30: 514.3 vs 200.0), so Epley is actually the more conservative (lower) of the two at higher rep counts.
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** Keep Epley as the default (it is a legitimate, widely-validated, real-world standard, correctly attributed) but: (1) cap or flag e1RM confidence for reps>10-12 (e.g. display 'low confidence' or widen an error band) since cross-formula divergence exceeds 50% by 20 reps; (2) note in-app that the reps=1 shortcut is an intentional override, not literal Epley (raw formula gives 103.3% of weight at 1 rep, not 100%); (3) consider exercise-aware adjustment or at least a disclaimer that deadlift e1RM is systematically underestimated and squat e1RM is less reliable at higher reps than bench press, per LeSuer et al. (1997) and Ware et al. (1995).

### C2 — Inverse Epley for rep targets  
*(Priority: Medium)*

- **Formula-check:** Algebra verified correct and exact. Epley: e1RM = weight*(1+reps/30). Solving for reps: e1RM/weight = 1+reps/30 -> reps = 30*(e1RM/weight - 1). Numerical round-trip check (weight=100, reps=1..50) confirms exact inverse with zero error at every point — this is pure algebra, not a new approximation, so it introduces no additional error beyond the Epley model itself. However, note the model's own error is NOT symmetric when inverted: Epley is a linear approximation of an empirically curvilinear reps-vs-%1RM relationship, so while the algebra is exact, the *reps* output inherits and can amplify the model's known rep-range-dependent bias (see applicability_limits).
- **Verified source(s):**
  - Epley, B. (1985). Poundage Chart. Boyd Epley Workout, University of Nebraska-Lincoln. — none (unpublished pamphlet)
    - *Verification:* Original source of formula; existence corroborated by multiple secondary sources, not independently located as a citable document
  - LeSuer DA, McCormick JH, Mayhew JL, Wasserstein RL, Arnold MD. The accuracy of prediction equations for estimating 1-RM performance. J Strength Cond Res. 1997;11(4):211-213. — referenced via secondary sources (OpenSIUC thesis, arxiv review)
    - *Verification:* Referenced consistently across multiple independent secondary sources (OpenSIUC validation thesis, review articles); found equations accurate at low rep counts, correlations >0.95 but exercise-dependent absolute error
  - Arvo/Epley formula summary and multiple validation-study reviews indicating Epley formula is most accurate in 1-10 rep range with accuracy decreasing above 10 reps — n/a (aggregator page, not primary literature)
    - *Verification:* Secondary/tertiary source; directionally consistent with primary validation studies found (LeSuer 1997, Reynolds 2006, Mayhew 2008) but not itself a peer-reviewed source
- **Evidence level:** C   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** The forward Epley formula is best validated for predicting e1RM from sets of ≤10 reps; multiple independent validation studies (LeSuer et al. 1997; Reynolds et al. 2006; Mayhew et al. 2008, summarized consistently across secondary sources) report prediction accuracy degrading substantially above ~10 reps because the true reps-vs-%1RM relationship is curvilinear while Epley assumes linearity. When INVERTED to solve for a rep target at a given %1RM, this means: (1) for low %1RM targets (large e1RM/weight ratio → high predicted rep counts), the algebraically-correct reps figure corresponds to a region of the model with the largest known real-world error — i.e., the formula will systematically mis-predict how many reps are actually achievable at that load, generally in an exercise- and individual-dependent direction. (2) Error also varies substantially by exercise (deadlift/lower-body compound lifts show larger errors than bench press). (3) At the extreme, reps→∞ as weight→0, which is physiologically meaningless — Epley has 'no upper bound' unlike Brzycki (undefined at 37 reps), so very light target loads can generate implausibly large rep prescriptions. (4) Individual variation (fiber type, training history, fatigue tolerance) further widens error at high rep counts.
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** Keep the algebraic inversion (it is exact and requires no change), but cap or flag its use for target rep counts beyond ~12-15: display a lower-confidence indicator or fall back to empirical %1RM-rep tables (e.g., NSCA chart) for high-rep/low-%1RM targets rather than trusting the raw linear extrapolation. Consider offering the Brzycki formula (bounded, undefined at 37 reps) as a sanity check or cross-reference for higher-rep targets.

### C3 — %1RM → reps & training zones  
*(Priority: Medium)*

- **Formula-check:** Not a formula but a categorical mapping: ≥90% 1RM = Strength, 75-90% = Hypertrophy, <75% = Volume/technique. Cross-checked against the NSCA 'Training Load Chart' (adapted from Landers, J. 'Maximum based on reps,' NSCA J 6(6):60-61, 1984) and the NSCA Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning (4th ed.) Table 17.7 %1RM-reps correspondence: approximately 100%=1RM, 95%=2, 93%=3, 90%=4, 87%=5, 85%=6, 83%=7, 80%=8, 77%=9, 75%=10, 70%=12, 67%=15. Under this table, the app's ≥90% boundary corresponds to roughly 1-4 reps (broadly consistent with NSCA's 'basic strength phase': 80-95% 1RM, 2-6 reps), the 75-90% band corresponds to roughly 4-10 reps (broadly consistent with commonly-cited 6-12 rep hypertrophy range, though NSCA's own 'hypertrophy/strength-endurance phase' is defined more broadly as 50-75% 1RM for 8-20 reps in some editions/chapters), and <75% corresponds to >10 reps (roughly consistent with NSCA muscular-endurance framing, though NSCA endurance cutoffs are usually given nearer 67% rather than 75%). Overall the app's boundaries are a reasonable, if slightly compressed, approximation of NSCA-style tables rather than an exact reproduction (different NSCA chapters/editions use somewhat different cut points, e.g. 67% vs. 75% for the endurance/volume threshold).
- **Verified source(s):**
  - National Strength and Conditioning Association Training Load Chart, adapted from Landers, J. Maximum based on reps. NSCA J. 1984;6(6):60-61. — no DOI (organizational chart/trade publication)
    - *Verification:* Verified — chart is publicly hosted by NSCA (nsca.com) and cites Landers 1984 as origin
  - National Strength and Conditioning Association. Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning (4th ed.), Table 17.7 — %1RM-to-reps correspondence table. — ISBN-based textbook, no DOI
    - *Verification:* Verified as existing (referenced/described consistently by independent secondary sources, e.g. CSCS exam-prep sites), though exact table values not independently confirmed against original textbook page
  - Refalo MC, Helms ER, Trexler ET, Hamilton DL, Fyfe JJ. Influence of Resistance Training Proximity-to-Failure on Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review with Meta-analysis. Sports Med. 2023;53(3):649-665. — 10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y
    - *Verification:* Verified via PubMed/PMC — supports that proximity-to-failure, not specific %1RM zone, is the more important hypertrophy driver
  - Schoenfeld BJ, Grgic J, Ogborn D, Krieger JW. Strength and Hypertrophy Adaptations Between Low- vs. High-Load Resistance Training: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. J Strength Cond Res. 2017;31(12):3508-3523. — PMID: 28834797
    - *Verification:* Verified via PubMed — found no significant hypertrophy difference between <60% and >65% 1RM loads when volume/effort matched
- **Evidence level:** D   **Confidence:** medium
- **Applicability limits:** The underlying %1RM-to-reps correspondence varies substantially by exercise (compound vs. isolation, upper vs. lower body), training status, and individual fiber-type/fatigue tolerance — NSCA-type tables are population averages for barbell compound lifts and are known to differ for machines/single-joint work. More importantly, the rigid zone *labels* (Strength/Hypertrophy/Volume) overstate how load-specific hypertrophy is: current dose-response and proximity-to-failure meta-analyses show muscle growth occurs across a broad range of loads/reps (roughly 30-90%+ 1RM, 5-35 reps) provided sets are taken near failure and volume is comparable, so labeling only 75-90% as 'Hypertrophy' is a simplification of the modern literature (which the same app tracker presumably references in a related claim on rep-range flexibility).
- **Verdict:** Simplification
- **Recommendation:** Keep the %1RM-rep correspondence numbers (they reasonably approximate NSCA-style tables) but soften the zone *labels*: reframe 'Hypertrophy' zone as one of several effective ranges rather than the exclusive hypertrophy range, and note that sets taken close to failure at <75% or >90% 1RM also drive hypertrophy. Consider citing 'effort/proximity-to-failure' alongside load as a determinant, per Refalo et al. 2023.

### C4 — Rep range ↔ training goal mapping  
*(Priority: High)*

- **Formula-check:** Not a mathematical formula but a categorical mapping (rep-range -> training goal). No arithmetic to reproduce; assessed against the definitional claim: Strength=4-6 reps, Hypertrophy=6-15 reps (compound 6-10 / isolation 10-15).
- **Verified source(s):**
  - Schoenfeld BJ, Grgic J, Ogborn D, Krieger JW (2017). Strength and Hypertrophy Adaptations Between Low- vs. High-Load Resistance Training: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. J Strength Cond Res 31(12):3508-3523. — 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200 / PMID 28834797
    - *Verification:* Verified — DOI, PMID, authors, title, journal, and abstract content all confirmed via PubMed and JSCR/LWW listing. Confirmed conclusion: maximal strength benefits favor heavy loads, while muscle hypertrophy can be equally achieved across a spectrum of loading ranges.
  - Ratamess NA, Alvar BA, Evetovich TK, et al. (2009). American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults. Med Sci Sports Exerc 41(3):687-708. — 10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670 / PMID 19204579
    - *Verification:* Verified via PubMed listing. Content matches claim's implied source: recommends 70-85% 1RM / 8-12 reps for novice-intermediate hypertrophy, 70-100%/1-12 reps (mostly 6-12RM) for advanced, i.e. narrower than what the app calls 'hypertrophy' range but still centered similarly.
  - ACSM Position Stand update (Overview of Reviews, synthesizing 137 systematic reviews, updating the 2009 position stand) — PMID 41843416 (per PubMed search result)
    - *Verification:* Verified as real, current (2025-2026) update — explicitly states hypertrophy is no longer confined to 8-12 reps and gives an acceptable load range of 30-100% 1RM for hypertrophy, directly supporting that a fixed narrow rep-range mapping is an outdated simplification.
- **Evidence level:** B   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** The 2017 Schoenfeld/Grgic meta-analysis criteria required ALL sets be taken to momentary muscular failure -- the hypertrophy-across-a-wide-rep-range finding assumes proximity to failure; if the app's plan generation doesn't also encode 'train close to failure,' the rep-range alone is an incomplete predictor of hypertrophy outcome. Strength (1RM) adaptation is genuinely more load/specificity-dependent -- heavy loads (>=80-85% 1RM, low reps) reliably produce superior 1RM gains versus light-load training (this part of the claim is well supported, evidence A/B). However hypertrophy is demonstrated across a much wider range than 6-15 reps -- studies with loads as low as 30-50% 1RM (often 20-30+ reps to failure) produce comparable hypertrophy to heavy loads in both trained and untrained populations. The newest ACSM update (updating the 2009 position stand) explicitly widens the hypertrophy load range to 30-100% 1RM, i.e., roughly 5 to 30+ reps. The compound-vs-isolation 6-10/10-15 split is a common coaching convention (joint stress, fatigue management) but is not itself derived from a dedicated RCT/meta-analysis distinguishing compound vs isolation optimal rep ranges — it is a reasonable practical heuristic, not a directly-evidenced rule.
- **Verdict:** Simplification
- **Recommendation:** Reframe the app's hypertrophy rep range as a practical default (e.g., 'hypertrophy training is commonly programmed at 6-15 reps, but is achievable from ~5 to 30+ reps provided sets are taken close to failure — RIR 0-3'), rather than presenting 6-15 as a hard boundary. Add a near-failure/effort qualifier (RIR/RPE) to the hypertrophy definition, since load range without effort context is not what the evidence actually supports. Keep the 4-6 rep strength recommendation largely as-is (better evidenced) but note the recent ACSM update lists >=80% 1RM as the modern strength-load target rather than a rep count alone.

### C5 — 10–20 working sets/muscle/week  
*(Priority: High)*

- **Formula-check:** Not a formula — a volume-dose recommendation range (10-20 working sets/muscle/week). Checked the underlying dose-response literature's reported set thresholds and effect sizes across volume bins.
- **Verified source(s):**
  - Schoenfeld BJ, Ogborn D, Krieger JW (2017). Dose-response relationship between weekly resistance training volume and increases in muscle mass: A systematic review and meta-analysis. J Sports Sci 35(11):1073-1082. — 10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197 / PMID 27433992
    - *Verification:* Verified via PubMed (title/authors/journal/year/DOI match). Confirmed content: 34 treatment groups from 15 studies; found a significant, graded dose-response effect of weekly sets on hypertrophy, with the highest studied volume category (>=10 sets/week) showing greater gains (~9.8% muscle thickness increase) than <5 sets/week (~5.4%) and 5-9 sets/week (~6.6%) bins, per secondary reporting of the meta-regression results.
  - Baz-Valle E, Fontes-Villalba M, Santos-Concejero J (2021). Total Number of Sets as a Training Volume Quantification Method for Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review. J Strength Cond Res 35(3):870-878. — 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002776
    - *Verification:* Verified — DOI, title, journal, year confirmed via PubMed listing referenced in a 2025 Sports Medicine dose-response paper's citation list.
  - Buckner S, Moreno E, Baxter HT (2023). The dose-response relationship between resistance training volume and muscle hypertrophy: there are still doubts. J Trainology 12(2):29-36. — 10.17338/trainology.12.2_29
    - *Verification:* Verified real and on-topic — a direct methodological critique of the volume dose-response claims, confirming the upper-bound of the volume-response curve is contested in the literature.
  - Israetel M, Hoffmann J, Smith CW. Scientific Principles of Hypertrophy Training. Renaissance Periodization (self-published guide, various editions from 2017). — no DOI — self-published coaching material
    - *Verification:* Verified as a real, correctly-attributed source, but confirmed NOT peer-reviewed — MEV/MAV/MRV terminology originates from Renaissance Periodization commercial coaching content, not from a journal article.
- **Evidence level:** B   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** The lower bound (~10 sets/week associated with near-maximal average gains in the meta-regression) has reasonable meta-analytic support, but Schoenfeld et al. (2017) themselves note this was the highest volume CATEGORY actually tested by the included trials — few studies examined truly high volumes (>20 sets/week), so the upper end of the app's 10-20 range and any implied 'ceiling' benefit above 20 is poorly characterized empirically, not a hard finding. Individual variability in volume tolerance/response is large and not captured by a single fixed range; training status (untrained vs. trained), muscle group, and recovery capacity all shift the effective range. Set-counting methodology matters a great deal: the underlying meta-analyses generally counted sets performed to or near momentary muscular failure ('hard sets') — an app that counts ALL logged sets (including warm-ups, low-effort or far-from-failure sets) will systematically overcount effective volume relative to what the evidence measured. The MEV/MRV framework is a popular, internally-consistent coaching heuristic built loosely on this literature but is itself not a peer-reviewed, independently validated construct — it should be flagged as practitioner-derived, not as an equivalent evidence tier to the Schoenfeld meta-analyses.
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** Keep 10-20 sets/week as a reasonable evidence-informed default range, but (1) explicitly label it as bounded on solid evidence at the lower/middle end (~10 sets ~ near-maximal average response) and much softer/more speculative at the upper end (15-20+), since few RCTs tested that high; (2) add a note that these figures assume sets performed within ~0-3 reps of failure — if the app counts all logged working sets indiscriminately, consider distinguishing 'hard sets' (close to failure) from total logged sets, or discount low-effort sets in volume calculations; (3) label MEV/MAV/MRV terminology, if used in-app, as a practitioner framework (Renaissance Periodization) rather than as a peer-reviewed classification.

### C6 — Double progression scheme  
*(Priority: Medium)*

- **Formula-check:** Not a mathematical formula; it is a conditional rule: IF reps at top of prescribed range for target sets THEN increase weight ELSE add +1 rep next session. Logically this is a valid, self-consistent operationalization of 'progressive overload' (a monotonically non-decreasing training-stimulus rule). No internal inconsistency found in the rule itself.
- **Verified source(s):**
  - American College of Sports Medicine. Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy Adults (Position Stand). Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2009;41(3):687-708. — 10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670
    - *Verification:* Verified — foundational evidence-graded position stand; grades general progressive-overload/periodization principles as Evidence Category A for several sub-components, but does not isolate 'double progression' as a distinct scheme with dedicated evidence grading
  - Legion Athletics / ACE Fitness descriptions of the double progression method (historical attribution to Alan Calvert, 1911 pamphlet). — n/a (trade/commercial sources, not peer-reviewed)
    - *Verification:* Verified as a real, long-standing coaching convention; no peer-reviewed head-to-head trial of double progression vs. alternative progression models (straight sets, wave loading, %-based periodization) was found
- **Evidence level:** D   **Confidence:** medium
- **Applicability limits:** Progressive overload itself is well-evidenced as necessary for continued strength/hypertrophy adaptation (ACSM position stand, Evidence Category A for related sub-components such as volume and load progression). However, no comparative RCTs were found that test double progression head-to-head against straight-weight progression, wave/undulating loading, or %-based (percentage-of-1RM) periodization for strength or hypertrophy outcomes. Double progression is best suited to moderate-to-high rep ranges (roughly 6-20 reps) common in hypertrophy-style training; it is less commonly used, and less well-matched, to low-rep (1-5) maximal-strength work, where %-based or RPE/RIR-based periodization is more standard practice and has more direct periodization-literature support (e.g., autoregulated/velocity-based load prescription reviews).
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** State clearly (in-app or in documentation) that double progression is a practical, widely-used convention for implementing progressive overload rather than an empirically-proven-superior scheme; no RCT evidence was found showing it outperforms alternatives. Consider allowing users training primarily in low-rep (1-5) ranges to opt into %-based or RPE-based progression instead, since double progression's assumptions fit hypertrophy-range training better than maximal-strength training.

### C7 — Rep-caps & weight increments  
*(Priority: Low)*

- **Formula-check:** Internally consistent design: rep-cap 8 for compound lifts, 12 for isolation lifts, triggering a weight increase once the top of the rep range is hit across all working sets (double progression). Weight-increment schedule (+2.5kg at >=40kg working weight, +1kg at 15-40kg, +0.5kg at <15kg) is a tiered percentage-based step-down that keeps jumps roughly proportional (6.25% at 40kg, up to 6.7% at 15kg, up to 3.3%+ at <15kg) rather than a flat absolute increment across all loads -- logically sound design intent (bigger absolute jumps only at loads where they represent a small relative increase).
- **Verified source(s):** none applicable (logical/consistency check, not a literature-dependent claim)
- **Evidence level:** D   **Confidence:** medium
- **Applicability limits:** Rep caps: an 8-rep ceiling for compounds and 12 for isolation is a common and defensible double-progression convention, but not the only valid one -- many programs (e.g. hypertrophy-oriented compound work) run compounds to 8-12 or even higher, so 8 is a strength/moderate-hypertrophy-biased choice, not a universal standard. Weight increments: the 2.5kg step at >=40kg matches the de facto commercial-gym floor (smallest standard plate is typically 1.25kg/side, i.e. a 2.5kg total minimum jump), so it is realistic without any microloading. However, the 1kg and especially 0.5kg steps below 40kg require fractional/micro-plates (0.25-1kg) that are common in powerlifting/Olympic-lifting gyms and specialty home setups but are not standard equipment in most commercial gyms -- a lifter without microplates cannot physically execute a barbell 0.5kg or 1kg jump. The increments are realistic for dumbbell racks with fine spacing, adjustable dumbbells, cable/pin-loaded machines with small plate increments, and any barbell setup with fractional plates; they are optimistic for a standard barbell in a typical commercial gym lacking microplates.
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** Add an in-app note/tooltip that the finer (1kg/0.5kg) increments assume access to fractional plates or small-increment equipment (dumbbells, pin-loaded machines, cable stacks), and that barbell users without microplates may need to round to the nearest available 1.25kg-plate jump (2.5kg total). Optionally let users override rep caps per program goal (e.g. allow up to 12 on compounds for hypertrophy-focused users).

### C8 — Working-weight formula (88% buffer + linear progression)  
*(Priority: High)*

- **Formula-check:** working_weight = e1RM / (1 + reps_lowend/30) * 0.88. Reproduced: this de-normalizes e1RM back to weight-at-reps_lowend via inverse Epley, then applies a flat 0.88 multiplier. Computed the effective %e1RM this yields across common rep_lowend values: reps_lowend=4 -> 77.6% of e1RM; reps_lowend=5 -> 75.4%; reps_lowend=6 -> 73.3%; reps_lowend=8 -> 69.5%; reps_lowend=10 -> 66.0%. So the formula effectively prescribes a working weight around 66-78% of e1RM depending on the target rep range, decreasing as reps_lowend increases (since Epley's own inverse already produces a lighter weight for higher reps, and the flat 0.88 buffer is then layered on top).
- **Verified source(s):**
  - Helms ER, Cronin J, Storey A, Zourdos MC (2016). Application of the Repetitions in Reserve-Based Rating of Perceived Exertion Scale for Resistance Training. Strength & Conditioning Journal 38(4):42-49. — cited via journals.lww.com/nsca-scj listing

    - *Verification:* Verified — full abstract/content retrieved from frontiersin.org; confirms RIR/RPE-based autoregulation as an alternative, individualized method to fixed %1RM prescription, and that day-to-day 1RM fluctuation undermines fixed-percentage prescription.
  - Zourdos MC et al. (2016) original RIR-based RPE scale paper (cited secondarily in multiple retrieved sources) — not independently retrieved this session
    - *Verification:* Referenced consistently across 3+ independent retrieved sources (JSCR 2019 Ormsbee et al., Frontiers 2018 Helms et al., MASS Research Review); content (RPE10=RIR0 scale, strong inverse correlation with bar velocity) is corroborated, medium-high confidence without primary-text confirmation.

- **Applicability limits:** 0.88 is a plausible, moderate 'top-set buffer' below the theoretical e1RM/estimated-max-for-reps value (roughly equivalent to leaving ~2-4 reps in reserve at the bottom of most rep ranges per Helms-style RIR/%1RM tables), but it is a single fixed constant applied uniformly, which the RPE/RIR literature argues is exactly the wrong approach: Helms et al. A rigid weekly linear increment for intermediate/advanced users risks premature plateaus, missed reps, or overreaching before the block ends.
- **Verdict:** Simplification
- **Recommendation:** (1) Reframe 0.88 explicitly as a heuristic top-set intensity buffer (not an evidence-derived constant) and consider making it configurable or auto-regulated via RPE/RIR feedback (e.g., let logged RIR/RPE on recent sets adjust the multiplier) rather than a single fixed value for all users. (2) Gate the linear +2.5kg/+1.25kg weekly increment scheme behind a detected/declared training-experience level: keep it for novices, but for intermediate/advanced users switch to slower progression (e.g., every 2-4 weeks, or percentage-based/RPE-based auto-regulation, or block/undulating periodization) to match established periodization practice and avoid unrealistic linear extrapolation over a 6-12 week block.

### C10 — Plateau & trend detection (0.2% / 3-week window)  
*(Priority: Medium)*

- **Formula-check:** Plateau flag: max(e1RM, last 3 weeks) ≤ 1.002 × max(e1RM, prior 3 weeks) → flagged as plateau (i.e., ≤0.2% improvement over a 3-week-vs-3-week comparison). This is a valid arithmetic comparison, but the 0.2% threshold is far below the known measurement noise floor for (e1RM-estimated) 1RM values.
- **Verified source(s):**
  - Grgic J, Lazinica B, Schoenfeld BJ, Pedisic Z. Test-Retest Reliability of the One-Repetition Maximum (1RM) Strength Assessment: A Systematic Review. Sports Med Open. 2020;6:31. — 10.1186/s40798-020-00260-z
    - *Verification:* Verified — systematic review of 32 studies (pooled n=1595): test-retest ICCs ranged 0.64-0.99 (median 0.97); coefficients of variation (CV) ranged 0.5-12.1% (median CV=4.2%), across trained and untrained, single- and multi-joint exercises
- **Evidence level:** A   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** The Grgic et al. 2020 systematic review characterizes DIRECTLY-TESTED 1RM test-retest variability (median CV 4.2%, range 0.5-12.1%). The app is using e1RM (formula-ESTIMATED, not directly tested, 1RM), which carries additional prediction error on top of this test-retest noise (validation studies summarized under C2 show Epley/Brzycki-type formulas have absolute errors that are 'quite high' even at their best, growing further above ~10 reps) — so the true noise floor for a rolling e1RM series is very likely equal to or larger than the ~4% median CV for directly-tested 1RM, not smaller. A 0.2% threshold is roughly 20-fold smaller than the median CV and far smaller even than the best-case (0.5%) reported CV in the literature, meaning ordinary session-to-session noise (e.g., a session at the top vs. bottom of the CV range) will very frequently produce spurious 'plateau' (or false 'trend') signals even when a lifter is progressing normally. Using the single highest e1RM within each 3-week window (a max-of-few-sessions statistic) further increases susceptibility to one noisy high reading dominating the window.
- **Verdict:** Simplification
- **Recommendation:** Raise the plateau threshold to reflect known measurement noise — e.g., require the two windows' max e1RM to differ by less than roughly the exercise's own historical CV (a reasonable default given the literature would be ~3-5%, not 0.2%) before flagging a plateau. Better: instead of comparing two single rolling maxima, fit a linear regression/trend line across all logged e1RM points over a longer window (e.g., 6-8 weeks) and test whether the slope's confidence interval excludes zero, which is more robust to single noisy sessions and gives a statistically defensible 'trend' rather than a brittle two-point comparison.

*(Priority: High)*

- **Formula-check:** Not a mathematical formula but fixed bodyweight-multiple thresholds per sex/lift/level (Beginner...Elite). No computation to reproduce; assessed against known methodology of commercial strength standard sites and against the statistical properties of raw bodyweight ratios vs. allometric/polynomial scaling.
- **Verified source(s):**
  - strengthlevel.com — 'Weightlifting Strength Standards' methodology page — n/a (commercial website, not peer-reviewed)
    - *Verification:* Verified as a real, currently operating source; site states its standards are built from '195,513,376 lifts from 27,893,268 Strength Level users' -- i.e., self-reported, non-random, non-peer-reviewed community-submitted data, not a scientific sample.
  - ExRx.net strength standards tables (referenced by multiple secondary calculator sites as 'ExRx.net Press Strength Standards, ages 18-39') — n/a (commercial/educational website)
    - *Verification:* Referenced consistently across independent secondary sources as a norm-based, non-peer-reviewed tabulation; original derivation methodology not independently confirmed this session but its existence and general content (age-banded, non-allometric ratio tables) is corroborated by multiple citing sources.
  - Efficiency of the Wilks and IPF Formulas at Comparing Maximal Strength Regardless of Bodyweight through Analysis of the Open Powerlifting Database — not independently confirmed via DOI this session (found via ResearchGate listing)
    - *Verification:* Verified existence/topic match via ResearchGate abstract; corroborates that raw bodyweight-ratio comparisons are recognized in the field as biased and that allometric formulas (Wilks, DOTS, IPF GL, using bodyweight^~0.65-0.67 scaling) were specifically developed to correct this bias, and that Wilks itself was later found to systematically underscore heavier lifters, prompting the IPF's 2020 adoption of the newer Goodlift/GL formula.
- **Evidence level:** D   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** The bodyweight-ratio thresholds used in this and comparable apps trace to commercial/coaching sources (StrengthLevel, ExRx.net, Lon Kilgore's standards) built from self-reported or convenience-sampled lifter databases, not peer-reviewed, randomly-sampled, or methodologically audited population studies -- classification as 'Beginner...Elite' reflects percentile position within a self-selected population of people who submit lifts to these platforms/apps, which skews toward more dedicated, self-selected lifters and is NOT representative of the general population. More fundamentally, a flat bodyweight-multiple ratio (e.g., 'squat 1.5x bodyweight = Intermediate') is well-established in the sports-science and powerlifting-scoring literature to be systematically biased across the bodyweight spectrum: strength scales with bodyweight non-linearly (empirically close to bodyweight^0.65-0.67, reflecting that muscle cross-sectional area scales with the square while mass scales with the cube of a linear body dimension), so a fixed ratio unfairly favors lighter lifters and unfairly penalizes heavier lifters relative to their true relative capability (or vice versa depending on how the ratio table was fit) -- exactly the miscalibration that led competitive powerlifting to replace the flawed Wilks formula (which itself under-scored heavier lifters) with the IPF Goodlift/GL formula in 2020, and that motivates DOTS as an alternative. The claim's thresholds also do not account for age-related strength decline; masters/older lifters are compared against the same fixed young-adult-derived thresholds, which will systematically misclassify older lifters as weaker/lower-level than their age-adjusted true standing.
- **Verdict:** Simplification
- **Recommendation:** Retain simple bodyweight-ratio tables for user-facing intuitiveness (they are easy to understand and are the de facto industry norm popularized by StrengthLevel/ExRx/Kilgore), but: (1) clearly label them as derived from self-reported/commercial-platform data, not a peer-reviewed or representative population sample; (2) consider offering an allometric-adjusted or DOTS/IPF-GL-based alternative view for users who want a bodyweight-fair comparison, since flat ratios are known to be biased across the weight spectrum (favoring one end depending on how the underlying table was fit); (3) add an age-adjustment option or at minimum a disclaimer that standards reflect young-adult (often 18-39) norms and will systematically under-rate older lifters relative to age-matched peers.

### C12 — Binary sex, no age correction  
*(Priority: Low)*

- **Formula-check:** Logical/consistency check only (no formula). The claim correctly identifies that the app's strength-standards feature uses a binary male/female selector with no age adjustment, and flags this as a limitation rather than asserting it is an error -- self-consistent framing.
- **Verified source(s):** none applicable (logical/consistency check, not a literature-dependent claim)
- **Evidence level:** C   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** The underlying physiology is well established: population strength/performance data consistently show a peak in the 20s-30s followed by an age-related decline (documented across large observational/cross-sectional cohorts such as grip-strength and powerlifting-record datasets), and average strength differences by sex are well documented, but sex itself is a multidimensional (chromosomal, hormonal, morphological) variable, so a binary category is a simplification of a more continuous biological reality (e.g. hormonal status, training history, and body composition all modulate strength independent of the male/female label). Strength norms/percentiles derived from a binary, age-unadjusted model will misrepresent both younger/older lifters (whose expected values differ substantially from an all-ages pooled norm) and individuals whose hormonal profile doesn't match the binary assumption (e.g. post-menopausal, hormone-therapy, or intersex users).
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** Document this explicitly as a known limitation in the app's help/about text. If feasible, add an optional age bracket to the strength-standards lookup (even coarse decade bins meaningfully improve norm accuracy), and frame standards as 'population reference values' rather than definitive individual benchmarks.

### C13 — Volume = tonnage (load×reps)  
*(Priority: Medium)*

- **Formula-check:** Volume(tonnage) = Σ(load × reps) across sets — arithmetically correct and unambiguous as a workload/mechanical-work metric. Not in dispute mathematically; the question is whether it is the right proxy variable for hypertrophy dose-response.
- **Verified source(s):**
  - Schoenfeld BJ, Ogborn D, Krieger JW. Dose-response relationship between weekly resistance training volume and increases in muscle mass: A systematic review and meta-analysis. J Sports Sci. 2017;35(11):1073-1082. — 10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197 / PMID: 27433992
    - *Verification:* Verified via PubMed — analyzed volume as weekly SETS (not tonnage) as the continuous predictor, finding a significant dose-response effect of set count on hypertrophy
  - Baz-Valle E, Fontes-Villalba M, Santos-Concejero J. Total Number of Sets as a Training Volume Quantification Method for Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review. J Strength Cond Res. 2021;35(3):870-878. — 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002776 / cited via PubMed reference list
    - *Verification:* Verified via PubMed citation record — systematic review specifically evaluating 'number of sets' as the volume-quantification method for hypertrophy
  - Schoenfeld BJ, Grgic J, Ogborn D, Krieger JW. Strength and Hypertrophy Adaptations Between Low- vs. High-Load Resistance Training: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. J Strength Cond Res. 2017;31(12):3508-3523. — PMID: 28834797
    - *Verification:* Verified via PubMed — found no significant hypertrophy difference between high- and low-load training when sets/effort were equated, undermining load(and thus tonnage, which is load-weighted) as the key stimulus variable relative to sets taken near failure
- **Evidence level:** A   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** The claim that 'number of hard/effective sets' is a better hypertrophy-stimulus proxy than tonnage is indeed the current meta-analytic consensus in the Schoenfeld/Krieger/Baz-Valle line of research: because equal-tonnage protocols using very different loads (e.g., heavy-low-rep vs. light-high-rep, taken to comparable proximity-to-failure) tend to produce similar hypertrophy, tonnage does not scale linearly with hypertrophic stimulus the way sets-to-near-failure appears to. That said, tonnage/volume-load remains a legitimate and widely-used metric for OTHER purposes: tracking overall mechanical work, monitoring training-load progression and fatigue/readiness over time, and correlating with strength-specific (not purely hypertrophy) adaptations, where heavier loads do show some advantage. The app's design — using SETS for per-muscle-group prescription (aligned with current hypertrophy literature) and TONNAGE for an overall 'Total volume' workload chart — is internally consistent with the literature, provided the tonnage chart is not presented as a hypertrophy-stimulus indicator in its own right.
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** No change needed to keep both metrics; sets-based tracking is already the more literature-aligned metric for per-muscle-group prescription, and tonnage is defensible as a general workload/progress-tracking indicator. Consider adding a brief in-app note clarifying that 'Total volume' (tonnage) reflects overall training load/work done rather than the primary driver of muscle growth, to prevent users from over-indexing on tonnage as a hypertrophy metric.

### C14 — Bodyweight/cardio = 0kg volume  
*(Priority: Low)*

- **Formula-check:** Design-consistency check: tonnage (volume load) is defined as weight x reps x sets, so an exercise with no external load (bodyweight movement, cardio) legitimately contributes 0kg to that metric -- this is a correct, not distorted, application of the tonnage formula. Per-muscle-group SET COUNTING is a separate metric that increments by 1 per completed set regardless of load, so a bodyweight set (e.g. bodyweight dips, push-ups) still counts toward that muscle group's weekly set total. The two metrics are independent by construction, so 0kg tonnage does not zero out or otherwise distort set-count-based volume-landmark tracking (e.g. MEV/MAV/MRV-style set targets).
- **Verified source(s):** none applicable (logical/consistency check, not a literature-dependent claim)
- **Evidence level:** D   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** This holds only as long as the two metrics are actually implemented independently in code (set counter reads from a rep/set field, tonnage reads from weight*reps*sets) -- if any downstream feature accidentally weights or filters 'sets' by nonzero tonnage (e.g. a volume dashboard that silently excludes 0kg entries), that would reintroduce the distortion the claim is checking for. Cardio activities, if logged without a discrete 'sets' field at all, may not enter the muscle-group set counter in the first place, which is a separate, correct behavior (cardio isn't resistance-training volume) rather than a bug caused by the 0kg convention.
- **Verdict:** Correct
- **Recommendation:** None for the underlying logic. As a safeguard, add a unit/regression test confirming set-count aggregation never filters on tonnage > 0, so a future refactor can't silently couple the two metrics.

### C15 — e1RM as universal cross-lift score  
*(Priority: Low)*

- **Formula-check:** Common e1RM formulas (Epley: 1RM = w(1 + r/30); Brzycki: 1RM = w x 36/(37-r); etc.) are regression fits calibrated primarily on low-to-moderate rep tests and are algebraically undefined or degenerate at very high r (Brzycki approaches a singularity as r -> 37); using a single formula as a universal cross-exercise 'score' assumes the load-rep relationship is uniform across lifts and rep ranges, which is not the case.
- **Verified source(s):**
  - NORMA Athletics -- '1RM Formulas Explained' (web article; states accuracy drops significantly above 10 reps, formulas not built for endurance ranges)
    - *Verification:* web source consulted directly; secondary/non-peer-reviewed, but consistent across multiple independent web sources
  - Medium article, 'Estimated 1RM vs True 1RM: What's the Difference?' -- references an unspecified 2016 accuracy study of Epley/Brzycki formulas in the 2-10 rep range, but gives no journal name, authors, or DOI
    - *Verification:* unverified -- secondary blog source makes an uncited claim about a '2016 meta-analysis'; no primary study was located or confirmed, so this attribution should NOT be treated as a verified peer-reviewed citation
- **Evidence level:** B   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** Validation studies and reviews consistently report formulas are most reliable in roughly the 1-10 rep range (best in ~2-6 reps, within about 3-7% of measured 1RM) and diverge substantially above 10 reps as fatigue and muscular-endurance capacity increasingly confound the load-rep relationship (multiple secondary web summaries state that accuracy drops significantly above 10 reps since the formulas were not built for endurance ranges, and report that well-validated formulas like Epley and Brzycki predicted 1RM within roughly 5-10% of measured 1RM for 2-10 rep tests -- these are secondary/blog-level sources, not a peer-reviewed meta-analysis verified directly by this review). For technical/skill-limited lifts (Olympic clean/snatch/jerk, and to a lesser extent complex multi-joint variants), the ceiling on performance is often technique/mobility/timing rather than pure muscular strength, so an e1RM extrapolated from submaximal reps on those lifts conflates skill acquisition with strength, making cross-session or cross-lift e1RM comparisons less meaningful than for simple, technique-stable lifts (e.g. leg press, machine rows).
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** Flag e1RM values computed from sets >10-12 reps as lower-confidence/estimated in the UI (e.g. a caveat icon), and consider excluding or separately labeling Olympic/technical lifts from the e1RM 'score' leaderboard, or adding a technique-limited flag so users don't over-interpret e1RM trends for those movements.

### C16 — Muscle-group classification by name (muscleOf())  
*(Priority: Low)*

- **Formula-check:** Consistency check on a name-string-matching classifier (muscleOf()) rather than a formula. Reviewed the four flagged edge cases against known biomechanics/EMG literature: (1) Dips -- correctly implicate both chest and triceps (torso-lean-dependent emphasis: more forward lean = more chest, more upright = more triceps), so a chest+triceps dual tag is appropriate, not a single-muscle tag. (2) Rows -- 'row' as a bare string is ambiguous: standard bent-over/seated/cable rows load lats/mid-back, but 'upright row' is a name-matching trap since it primarily targets deltoids/traps, not lats, despite sharing the word 'row'. (3) Rear-delt work (reverse fly, face pull, rear delt raise) should be tagged posterior deltoid/upper-back, distinctly from generic 'shoulders' which naive matching often collapses into anterior/medial-deltoid-dominant movements (e.g. overhead press). (4) Hip thrust -- EMG literature supports classifying it as primarily glute (and secondarily hamstring/biceps femoris) dominant, with comparatively little quadriceps involvement relative to squats.
- **Verified source(s):**
  - Comparison of Gluteus Maximus, Biceps Femoris, and Vastus Lateralis EMG Activity in the Back Squat and Barbell Hip Thrust Exercises (13 trained women, 10RM loads)
    - *Verification:* verified via web search of primary study results
  - Sci-Sport summary of MRI/EMG hypertrophy comparison: squats vs hip thrusts for glute/quad/adductor development
    - *Verification:* verified via web search, consistent with EMG study above
- **Evidence level:** C   **Confidence:** high
- **Applicability limits:** Direct EMG comparison data support the hip-thrust classification: a controlled study of trained women found that the barbell hip thrust elicited significantly greater mean and peak upper and lower gluteus maximus, and biceps femoris (hamstring) EMG activity than the back squat, while the back squat failed to elicit significantly greater vastus lateralis (quadriceps) EMG activity than the barbell hip thrust -- i.e. hip thrust is not quad-dominant and is at least comparably or more glute/hamstring-dominant than squats. A related comparison further reports squats outperform hip thrusts in quadriceps and adductor development, highlighting the squat's effectiveness in engaging a greater proportion of the thigh muscles, reinforcing that hip thrust should not be tagged quad-primary. Other naive-name-matching traps worth flagging beyond the four listed: 'close-grip bench press' (implies triceps emphasis over chest/shoulders relative to standard-grip bench), 'Romanian deadlift'/'stiff-leg deadlift' vs plain 'deadlift' (RDL is hamstring/glute-hinge dominant with minimal quad and back-extensor loading vs a conventional deadlift's fuller posterior-chain and back involvement), 'incline' vs 'decline' vs 'flat' press (shifts chest emphasis upper vs lower), 'sumo' vs 'conventional' deadlift (quad vs hip-hinge emphasis), and 'preacher curl'/'concentration curl' (biceps-only isolation) vs 'chin-up' (contains curl-adjacent flexion but is a compound back+biceps movement) -- all cases where a keyword substring match can mis-tag the dominant muscle group without an explicit exception list.
- **Verdict:** Correct-with-nuance
- **Recommendation:** Maintain an explicit exception/override table for name-matching traps (upright row, Romanian/stiff-leg deadlift, close-grip/decline/incline press variants, sumo vs conventional deadlift, preacher/concentration curl vs chin-up) rather than relying purely on substring matches on 'row', 'deadlift', 'press', 'curl'. Support multi-muscle-group tags with primary/secondary weighting (as already done conceptually for dips) so hip thrust, rows, and rear-delt work aren't forced into a single dominant-muscle bucket.

### Medium priority

6. **C3 (%1RM → reps & training zones)** — Keep the %1RM-rep correspondence numbers (they reasonably approximate NSCA-style tables) but soften the zone *labels*: reframe 'Hypertrophy' zone as one of several effective ranges rather than the exclusive hypertrophy range, and note that sets taken close to failure at <75% or >90% 1RM also drive hypertrophy. Consider citing 'effort/proximity-to-failure' alongside load as a determinant, per Refalo et al. 2023.
7. **C9 (Push/Pull volume balance)** — Frame this metric explicitly as a coaching heuristic/awareness tool, not an evidence-validated injury-prevention threshold (no RCT/cohort evidence exists for any specific ratio). Consider noting that most cited coaching guidance favors a pull-biased ratio (~2:1 pull:push) rather than 1:1 as ‘balanced’, and let users choose their target ratio rather than presenting 1:1 as the objectively correct balance point.
8. **C10 (Plateau & trend detection (0.2% / 3-week window))** — Raise the plateau threshold to reflect known measurement noise — e.g., require the two windows' max e1RM to differ by less than roughly the exercise's own historical CV (a reasonable default given the literature would be ~3-5%, not 0.2%) before flagging a plateau. Better: instead of comparing two single rolling maxima, fit a linear regression/trend line across all logged e1RM points over a longer window (e.g., 6-8 weeks) and test whether the slope's confidence interval excludes zero, which is more robust to single noisy sessions and gives a statistically defensible 'trend' rather than a brittle two-point comparison.
9. **C2 (Inverse Epley for rep targets)** — Keep the algebraic inversion (it is exact and requires no change), but cap or flag its use for target rep counts beyond ~12-15: display a lower-confidence indicator or fall back to empirical %1RM-rep tables (e.g., NSCA chart) for high-rep/low-%1RM targets rather than trusting the raw linear extrapolation. Consider offering the Brzycki formula (bounded, undefined at 37 reps) as a sanity check or cross-reference for higher-rep targets.
10. **C6 (Double progression scheme)** — State clearly (in-app or in documentation) that double progression is a practical, widely-used convention for implementing progressive overload rather than an empirically-proven-superior scheme; no RCT evidence was found showing it outperforms alternatives. Consider allowing users training primarily in low-rep (1-5) ranges to opt into %-based or RPE-based progression instead, since double progression's assumptions fit hypertrophy-range training better than maximal-strength training.
11. **C13 (Volume = tonnage (load×reps))** — No change needed to keep both metrics; sets-based tracking is already the more literature-aligned metric for per-muscle-group prescription, and tonnage is defensible as a general workload/progress-tracking indicator. Consider adding a brief in-app note clarifying that 'Total volume' (tonnage) reflects overall training load/work done rather than the primary driver of muscle growth, to prevent users from over-indexing on tonnage as a hypertrophy metric.

### Low priority

12. **C7 (Rep-caps & weight increments)** — Add an in-app note/tooltip that the finer (1kg/0.5kg) increments assume access to fractional plates or small-increment equipment (dumbbells, pin-loaded machines, cable stacks), and that barbell users without microplates may need to round to the nearest available 1.25kg-plate jump (2.5kg total). Optionally let users override rep caps per program goal (e.g. allow up to 12 on compounds for hypertrophy-focused users).
13. **C12 (Binary sex, no age correction)** — Document this explicitly as a known limitation in the app's help/about text. If feasible, add an optional age bracket to the strength-standards lookup (even coarse decade bins meaningfully improve norm accuracy), and frame standards as 'population reference values' rather than definitive individual benchmarks.
14. **C15 (e1RM as universal cross-lift score)** — Flag e1RM values computed from sets >10-12 reps as lower-confidence/estimated in the UI (e.g. a caveat icon), and consider excluding or separately labeling Olympic/technical lifts from the e1RM 'score' leaderboard, or adding a technique-limited flag so users don't over-interpret e1RM trends for those movements.
15. **C16 (Muscle-group classification by name (muscleOf()))** — Maintain an explicit exception/override table for name-matching traps (upright row, Romanian/stiff-leg deadlift, close-grip/decline/incline press variants, sumo vs conventional deadlift, preacher/concentration curl vs chin-up) rather than relying purely on substring matches on 'row', 'deadlift', 'press', 'curl'. Support multi-muscle-group tags with primary/secondary weighting (as already done conceptually for dips) so hip thrust, rows, and rear-delt work aren't forced into a single dominant-muscle bucket.
16. **C14 (Bodyweight/cardio = 0kg volume)** — None for the underlying logic. As a safeguard, add a unit/regression test confirming set-count aggregation never filters on tonnage > 0, so a future refactor can't silently couple the two metrics.
